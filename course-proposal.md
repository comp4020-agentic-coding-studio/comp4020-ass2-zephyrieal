# SLOP3XXX — Designing Things People Hate

## Course Proposal

### 1. Course Overview

**Designing Things People Hate** is a semester-long course about designing products, interfaces, services, and experiences that are technically functional but somehow make people miserable.

The course begins with a simple question:

> **If good design makes people's lives easier, what exactly makes bad design so effective at making people's lives worse?**

Students investigate bad interfaces, confusing systems, annoying physical products, manipulative apps, hostile public spaces, unnecessary bureaucracy, and deliberately frustrating experiences.

This is not simply a course about identifying ugly websites or making intentionally bad UI. A frustrating experience is rarely random. It usually comes from a decision about **what the system wants, what the user wants, and whose interests win**.

The course therefore progresses through:

**Notice → Explain → Manipulate → Break → Measure → Design → Defend → Redesign**

By the end of the semester, students should be capable of looking at an everyday system and explaining why it feels terrible, what mechanisms make it terrible, and how those mechanisms could be deliberately reproduced or removed.

---

## 2. The Central Question

The course revolves around one question:

> **How do you design something that people hate?**

At first, students may interpret this as a visual-design question:

- Make it ugly.
- Make the buttons confusing.
- Add pop-ups.
- Use terrible colours.
- Hide the important information.

The course gradually challenges this assumption.

A truly effective terrible design does not necessarily look terrible.

Some of the most frustrating systems are visually polished. They are frustrating because they:

- make users wait;
- remove choices;
- hide information;
- exploit expectations;
- create unnecessary work;
- punish mistakes;
- manipulate attention;
- optimise for the wrong person;
- or make the user feel like they are the problem.

The course therefore moves from **"bad-looking design"** to **"design that produces bad experiences."**

---

## 3. Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

1. Identify design decisions that create frustrating or harmful user experiences.
2. Analyse the psychological, behavioural, and structural mechanisms behind bad design.
3. Distinguish between accidental bad design and deliberately harmful design.
4. Design interfaces and systems that intentionally create specific undesirable behaviours.
5. Evaluate a design using observation, testing, and evidence.
6. Explain how business, institutional, and technical constraints influence design decisions.
7. Redesign a hostile or frustrating system while understanding what made it hostile in the first place.
8. Defend design decisions and recognise when "bad design" may actually be successful design for another stakeholder.

---

# 4. Semester Structure

The semester is divided into four stages.

| Stage | Weeks | Question |
|---|---:|---|
| **I. Seeing Bad Design** | 1–3 | What makes something hateable? |
| **II. Building Bad Design** | 4–7 | How can we deliberately create it? |
| **III. Breaking Bad Design** | 8–10 | How do we test and understand it? |
| **IV. Becoming the Designer** | 11–12 | Can we design, defend, and redesign it? |

The weeks are deliberately connected rather than functioning as twelve unrelated topics.

# 5. Weekly Curriculum

## Week 1 — What Makes You Hate Something?

### Theme

**Bad design is not the same as ugly design.**

The course starts with students bringing examples of things they hate using.

Examples might include:

- confusing ticket machines;
- terrible government websites;
- impossible-to-cancel subscriptions;
- printers;
- supermarket self-checkouts;
- parking machines;
- apps that constantly ask for notifications;
- elevators;
- doors that appear to work one way but actually work another.

Students initially classify their examples according to **what the user experiences**, rather than why it happens.

### Activity — The Hate Wall

Students submit an example of something they hate and explain the exact moment where the experience becomes frustrating.

The class begins building a shared **Hate Catalogue**.

### Key Question

> **What did the designer make the user do?**

### Builds From

This establishes the fundamental distinction between **something being ugly** and **something being badly designed**.

---

## Week 2 — The User Is Not the Customer

### Theme

**Someone designed the system. Someone else benefits from it.**

Students learn to separate:

- user goals;
- designer goals;
- business goals;
- institutional goals;
- technical constraints.

A system can therefore be "bad" for one person while being extremely successful for another.

For example, a cancellation process that takes fifteen minutes may be terrible for the customer but useful for a company trying to reduce cancellations.

### Activity — Follow the Incentive

For one example from the Hate Catalogue, students answer:

> **Who benefits from this being difficult?**

### Key Idea

> **Bad design often makes perfect sense when you look at who the system is designed for.**

### Builds From

Week 1 taught students **what they hate**.

Week 2 teaches them to ask **why it exists**.

---

## Week 3 — Your Brain Is Part of the Interface

### Theme

**Designers don't just design interfaces. They design decisions.**

Students are introduced to behavioural concepts such as:

- defaults;
- loss aversion;
- scarcity;
- social proof;
- commitment;
- attention;
- cognitive load;
- choice overload;
- anticipation;
- habit formation.

Students revisit their Hate Catalogue and identify the psychological mechanisms involved.

For example:

> "The website made me choose the expensive option."

becomes:

> "The website used visual emphasis and default selection to increase the probability of choosing the expensive option."

### Activity — Make Me Click It

Students receive a neutral interface and are asked to increase the probability that another student clicks one particular button.

They cannot add functionality.

They can only change the **design**.

### Builds From

Students now have:

**Experience → Stakeholder → Psychology**

The next question becomes:

> **What happens when we deliberately combine these things?**

---

## Week 4 — The Dark Arts of Interface Design

### Theme

**Dark patterns.**

Students investigate patterns such as:

- confirmshaming;
- hidden costs;
- forced continuity;
- bait and switch;
- trick questions;
- disguised advertisements;
- difficult cancellation;
- privacy manipulation;
- sneaking items into baskets.

The emphasis is not simply on memorising names. Students must identify **what behaviour each pattern is attempting to produce**.

### Activity — Dark Pattern Bingo

Students browse real interfaces and attempt to identify dark patterns.

For each pattern they identify, they must answer:

1. What is the user being encouraged to do?
2. What would the user probably do without the manipulation?
3. What design decision changes that behaviour?

### Builds From

Week 3 introduced behavioural influence.

Week 4 applies those ideas to real interface design.

---

## Week 5 — Make It Worse

### Theme

**Intentional bad design.**

Students are given a normal interface.

Their task:

> **Make it significantly worse without breaking it.**

They cannot simply remove functionality.

Instead, they must introduce specific forms of friction.

Examples include:

- increasing cognitive load;
- making important information harder to find;
- introducing ambiguity;
- creating unnecessary decisions;
- increasing waiting;
- making errors harder to recover from.

### Assessment 1 — The Worst Interface

Students submit:

1. Original interface
2. Redesigned interface
3. Design decisions
4. Intended user behaviour
5. Explanation of why each decision should make the experience worse

### Builds From

Weeks 1–4 were primarily analytical.

Week 5 is the first major **making** exercise.

Students move from:

> "I recognise bad design."

to:

> **"I can deliberately produce bad design."**

---

## Week 6 — Friction

### Theme

**Not all difficulty is equal.**

Students investigate the difference between:

- useful friction;
- accidental friction;
- deliberate friction;
- destructive friction.

A password requirement may protect an account.

The same requirement can become terrible when a system demands a password reset every week.

Students learn to think about friction as something that can be **designed and measured**.

### Activity — Friction Mapping

Students take a common task such as:

> Buy a ticket.

They map every step:

**Open → Search → Select → Enter Information → Pay → Confirm**

They then deliberately introduce friction into different parts of the journey.

The class compares which forms of friction are most annoying.

### Builds From

Week 5 taught students to make an interface worse.

Week 6 asks:

> **Which kind of worse?**

---

## Week 7 — The Physical World Is Also a UI

### Theme

**Bad design doesn't require a screen.**

Students leave the world of websites and apps.

They examine:

- doors;
- chairs;
- packaging;
- public transport;
- bathrooms;
- signs;
- vending machines;
- road layouts;
- furniture;
- kitchen appliances.

The central idea is that **affordances and expectations exist everywhere**.

### Activity — Hostile Object Safari

Students document five physical objects that communicate something incorrectly.

For each object:

> What does it look like you should do?

> What do you actually have to do?

> Why is the difference confusing?

### Builds From

Students expand their understanding of design from **software interfaces** to **human interaction with physical objects**.

---

## Week 8 — Bureaucracy: Designing the Impossible

### Theme

**The worst interface might be a process.**

Students examine systems where the interface is not a screen but a sequence of rules.

Examples include:

- university administration;
- government forms;
- insurance;
- refunds;
- applications;
- customer support.

The central idea:

> **A beautiful interface cannot save a terrible process.**

### Activity — The Maze

Students are given a simple objective:

> Change your address.

However, the system contains deliberately conflicting instructions, unnecessary forms, unclear terminology, and repeated information.

Students attempt to complete it.

They then analyse where the system creates failure.

### Builds From

Week 7 expands the definition of an interface.

Week 8 pushes this further by treating an **entire process as an interface**.

---

## Week 9 — Does It Actually Work?

### Theme

**Being annoying is not the same as being effective.**

Students test their bad designs.

Up to this point, students have been designing things that *should* be frustrating.

Now they need evidence.

They conduct small user tests and measure:

- completion time;
- number of errors;
- abandonment;
- confusion;
- frustration;
- success rate;
- number of clicks;
- perceived difficulty.

### Activity — Human Guinea Pigs

Students test their Week 5 interface on classmates.

The participant is not told what is being tested.

The designer observes silently.

The key question is:

> **Did the design actually produce the behaviour we predicted?**

### Builds From

This introduces empirical evaluation.

Students learn that designing something badly in theory does not necessarily mean that users will actually experience it as intended.

---

## Week 10 — When Bad Design Is Good Design

### Theme

**Is it actually bad?**

This is the course's main intellectual twist.

Students revisit everything they have learned.

A design may be frustrating but necessary.

Examples include:

- airport security;
- medical warnings;
- confirmation dialogs;
- speed bumps;
- two-factor authentication;
- safety interlocks.

Students debate:

> **If something annoys users but prevents harm, is it bad design?**

This introduces a more sophisticated definition:

> **Design quality depends on what behaviour the system is trying to produce and for whom.**

### Activity — The Defence

Each student is assigned a hated design from the Hate Catalogue.

Their task is to **defend it**.

They must argue:

> "This is actually good design."

Even if they personally hate it.

### Builds From

Students learn to distinguish:

**Bad experience ≠ bad design**

and

**Good experience ≠ good design**

---

## Week 11 — Fix It Without Breaking It

### Theme

**Redesign.**

Students return to their original bad system.

But now they must redesign it.

The challenge:

> **Make it better without destroying the original system's legitimate goals.**

Students must identify:

1. What the user needs.
2. What the organisation needs.
3. What constraints exist.
4. What was causing the original problem.
5. What they changed.
6. What trade-offs remain.

### Activity — The Redesign Review

Students present:

**Before → Why it was bad → What caused it → Redesign → What changed**

Peers critique whether the redesign actually solves the original problem.

### Builds From

This is the payoff from the entire semester.

Students can now move through:

**Observation → Psychology → Manipulation → Creation → Testing → Ethics → Redesign**

---

## Week 12 — The Worst Design Awards

### Theme

**What have we learned to hate?**

The final week is structured more like an awards ceremony than a normal lecture.

Possible categories include:

- Most Confusing
- Most Manipulative
- Most Unnecessary
- Most Beautifully Terrible
- Most Effective
- Most Defensible
- Worst Physical Object
- Worst Interface
- Worst Process

Students present their final projects.

The course ends by returning to the question from Week 1:

> **What makes something genuinely bad?**

There is deliberately no single answer.

---

# 6. How the Weeks Build Together

The course should feel like **one continuous investigation**, rather than twelve separate lectures.

The progression is:

```text
WEEK 1
"I hate this."
        ↓
WEEK 2
"Who designed it this way?"
        ↓
WEEK 3
"Why does it work on people?"
        ↓
WEEK 4
"What techniques are being used?"
        ↓
WEEK 5
"Can I deliberately make it worse?"
        ↓
WEEK 6
"What kind of friction am I creating?"
        ↓
WEEK 7
"Does this exist outside software?"
        ↓
WEEK 8
"What if the entire system is the interface?"
        ↓
WEEK 9
"Does my bad design actually work?"
        ↓
WEEK 10
"Is bad design always bad?"
        ↓
WEEK 11
"Can I fix it?"
        ↓
WEEK 12
"What does 'good design' actually mean?"
```

This progression creates a clear intellectual arc:

**Observe → Understand → Manipulate → Create → Test → Question → Redesign**

# 7. Assessment Structure

## Assessment 1 — Hate Catalogue — 15%

Throughout Weeks 1–4, students collect and analyse examples of bad design.

The catalogue contains:

- screenshots/photos;
- description;
- user problem;
- stakeholder;
- suspected intention;
- psychological mechanism;
- severity.

This becomes their personal dataset for the rest of the semester.

---

## Assessment 2 — The Worst Interface — 25%

Students deliberately design a terrible interface.

The goal is **not** to make something visually ugly.

The goal is to create a specific undesirable behaviour.

For example:

> Make users accidentally choose the wrong option.

or:

> Make users spend significantly longer completing the task.

or:

> Make users abandon the task.

Students must explain the mechanism behind their design.

---

## Assessment 3 — Hostile Design Experiment — 20%

Students conduct user testing on their bad design.

They determine:

- Did users behave as predicted?
- Which parts caused frustration?
- Which parts failed to produce the intended effect?
- What did users do that the designer did not anticipate?

This is where students learn that **people are surprisingly difficult to control**.

---

## Final Project — The Redemption Project — 40%

Students choose an existing hated system.

They produce five components.

### Part 1 — Autopsy

Explain why it is bad.

### Part 2 — Defence

Explain why someone might have designed it that way.

### Part 3 — Experiment

Test the original system.

### Part 4 — Redesign

Create a better version.

### Part 5 — Defence

Explain why the new design is actually better.

The final submission is therefore not simply:

> "Here is my nicer UI."

It is:

> **"Here is why the original system behaves the way it does, what I learned from testing it, and why my alternative is better."**

# 8. The Course Website

The course website itself should participate in the course rather than simply describe it.

The website can deliberately demonstrate the principles being taught.

## Homepage

A giant button:

> **ENTER COURSE**

The button subtly moves whenever the cursor approaches it.

---

## Navigation

Some pages have names that are almost, but not quite, what users expect.

For example:

```text
Assessments
Assessment Information
Assessment Information (Current)
Assessment Information FINAL
Assessment Information FINAL 2
```

This demonstrates poor information architecture.

---

## Enrolment

The website could contain a deliberately frustrating cancellation flow:

```text
Drop Course
     ↓
Are you sure?
     ↓
Are you REALLY sure?
     ↓
Before you leave, tell us why.
     ↓
Actually, you cannot leave.
```

This becomes a miniature demonstration of dark patterns.

---

## Course Calendar

Important events are visually tiny while irrelevant events are enormous.

Students must learn to find the actual information.

---

## Assessment Page

The important deadline is initially hidden beneath several expandable sections.

The website itself becomes an example of poor information hierarchy.

---

## Feedback

A button labelled:

> **Report a Problem**

leads to:

> We are glad you care about the course. Unfortunately, this form is currently unavailable.

# 9. Course Philosophy

The course should avoid becoming:

> "Here are 25 dark patterns. Memorise them."

Instead, it should have a stronger central thesis:

> **Design is the deliberate arrangement of choices.**

And therefore:

> **If you control the choices, you influence the behaviour.**

This makes the course broader than dark patterns.

It can explore:

- UX;
- psychology;
- physical design;
- bureaucracy;
- architecture;
- software;
- business;
- ethics;
- games;
- public infrastructure.

Students are not simply learning **how bad designers design**.

They are learning:

> **How design controls behaviour — and what happens when you use that power badly.**

---

# 10. Why This Works as a Slop University Course

A real university could plausibly teach:

- Human-Computer Interaction;
- UX Design;
- Interaction Design;
- Ethical Design.

But it would be much harder to justify an entire semester called:

> **Designing Things People Hate**

This makes the course niche enough for Slop University while still being academically coherent.

The humour comes from the **framing**, rather than making the educational content meaningless.

The course still teaches genuine skills:

- observation;
- behavioural analysis;
- design;
- prototyping;
- user testing;
- systems thinking;
- ethics;
- redesign.

The result is a course that sounds ridiculous at first, but becomes increasingly serious as the semester progresses.

---

# 11. One-Sentence Course Pitch

> **Designing Things People Hate teaches students how interfaces, objects, and systems manipulate human behaviour — by making them deliberately terrible, testing just how terrible they can become, and eventually learning how to fix them.**

# 12. Addendum — explicit assessment weeks

Each assessment needs one explicit due week, since the site models assessments
as discrete entries with a fixed `week`. This regroups the semester into four
three-week blocks, each closing with the assessment it built toward:

| Assessment | Due | Weight | Purpose |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Hate Catalogue | Week 3 | 15% | learn to observe and analyse bad design |
| The Worst Interface | Week 6 | 25% | deliberately create bad design |
| Hostile Design Experiment | Week 9 | 20% | test whether the bad design actually works |
| The Redemption Project (final) | Week 12 | 40% | analyse, defend, test and redesign a real system |

```text
Weeks 1–3   OBSERVE     "What makes this bad?"           → Assessment 1
Weeks 4–6   CREATE      "Can I make something worse?"    → Assessment 2
Weeks 7–9   TEST        "Does my bad design work?"        → Assessment 3
Weeks 10–12 QUESTION +  "Is it actually bad, and can I     → Final Assessment
            REDESIGN     do better?"
```

This supersedes the four-stage table in Section 4 and the "roughly when" of
Section 7's assessments; the weekly themes and activities in Section 5 are
unchanged, except for Weeks 10–12, which become the Redemption Project's own
workshop rather than new unrelated material:

- **Week 10 — Is Bad Design Actually Bad?** (was "When Bad Design Is Good
  Design"): students defend a hated design and identify legitimate reasons for
  frustrating design.
- **Week 11 — Fix It Without Breaking It**: students develop and critique
  their redesign (unchanged theme, now explicitly the final project's draft).
- **Week 12 — The Worst Design Awards**: final presentations double as the
  Redemption Project's presentation.
