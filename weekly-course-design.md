# Designing Things People Hate — Weekly Course Design

## Course Structure

Each week combines a **lecture** introducing the main concept with a **tutorial or workshop** where students apply it.

The course follows a three-week assessment cycle:

**Learn → Apply → Demonstrate**

The semester as a whole progresses through:

**Observe → Analyse → Create → Test → Question → Redesign**

---

## Week 1 — What Makes You Hate Something?

### Concept
**Frustration and usability**

### Lecture
The course begins by asking what makes something bad to use. Students examine the difference between something that looks bad and something that creates a bad experience.

Topics include:
- Expectations and feedback
- Discoverability
- Errors and recovery
- Confusing interactions
- Bad-looking vs bad-to-use

### Tutorial — The Hate Wall
Students bring three examples of things they hate using, such as websites, ticket machines, doors, printers, apps, or everyday objects.

For each example, they identify the exact interaction that causes frustration.

These examples are added to a shared **Hate Wall**, which is revisited throughout the semester.

### Key Question
> **What exactly makes this frustrating?**

---

## Week 2 — The User Is Not the Customer

### Concept
**Stakeholders and incentives**

### Lecture
Students examine why frustrating designs exist.

Topics include:
- User goals vs business goals
- Stakeholder conflicts
- Organisational incentives
- Intentional vs accidental bad design
- Who a system is actually designed for

### Tutorial — Follow the Incentive
Students select an example from the Hate Wall and map its stakeholders.

They identify:
- What the user wants
- What the organisation wants
- Where those goals conflict
- Who benefits from the current design

### Key Question
> **Who benefits from this being difficult?**

---

## Week 3 — Your Brain Is Part of the Interface

### Concept
**Behaviour and cognition**

### Lecture
Students examine how design influences decisions and behaviour.

Topics include:
- Cognitive load
- Defaults
- Attention
- Choice overload
- Loss aversion
- Visual hierarchy
- Social proof

### Tutorial — Make Me Click It
Students receive the same neutral interface and are assigned one option they want users to choose.

They cannot change the functionality. They can only change the presentation and interface design.

The class compares how different designs influence the same decision.

### Assessment
**Hate Catalogue — 15%**

Students submit a structured analysis of bad-design examples using concepts from Weeks 1–3.

---

# Week 4 — The Dark Arts of Interface Design

### Concept
**Manipulative design**

### Lecture
Students examine interfaces deliberately designed to influence users against their interests.

Topics include:
- Dark patterns
- Confirmshaming
- Obstruction
- Hidden costs
- Forced actions
- Sneaking
- Visual interference
- Difficult cancellation

The focus is not simply on naming patterns, but understanding the behaviour they attempt to produce.

### Tutorial — Dark Pattern Detective
Students find examples of manipulative interfaces and analyse:

1. What does the user want?
2. What does the organisation want?
3. What behaviour is the interface encouraging?
4. What design technique creates that behaviour?

### Key Question
> **How can an interface deliberately influence the wrong decision?**

---

## Week 5 — Make It Worse

### Concept
**Designing bad experiences**

### Lecture
Students move from analysing bad design to creating it.

Topics include:
- Creating confusion
- Poor information hierarchy
- Ambiguity
- Unnecessary steps
- Error-prone interactions
- Hiding important information

A key constraint is introduced:

> **The interface must still work.**

Simply breaking functionality does not count as bad design.

### Tutorial — Bad Design Studio
Students receive a simple, well-designed interface such as a checkout, booking system, registration form, or settings page.

Their task is to deliberately make it worse while keeping it functional.

### Key Question
> **Can you make something worse without breaking it?**

---

## Week 6 — Friction

### Concept
**Interaction cost**

### Lecture
Students examine friction as a deliberate design variable.

Topics include:
- Cognitive friction
- Physical friction
- Emotional friction
- Temporal friction
- Unnecessary steps
- Useful vs harmful friction

Students also encounter the idea that friction is not always bad.

### Tutorial — Friction Lab
Students redesign one simple task three ways:

1. Low-friction version
2. Annoying version
3. Extremely high-friction version

They compare how small design decisions increase the effort required to complete the same task.

### Assessment
**The Worst Interface — 25%**

Students submit a functional interface deliberately designed to create a specific frustrating experience.

---

# Week 7 — The Physical World Is Also a UI

### Concept
**Affordances and physical interaction**

### Lecture
The course expands beyond screens.

Students examine:
- Doors
- Controls
- Signs
- Packaging
- Ticket machines
- Vending machines
- Public spaces
- Everyday objects

The focus is on the difference between what an object appears to allow and what the user can actually do.

### Tutorial — Hostile Object Safari
Students find confusing physical designs around campus or in public spaces.

For each example, they document:

- What the object appears to communicate
- What the user expects to do
- What they actually need to do
- Why the mismatch causes confusion

### Key Question
> **What happens when an object communicates the wrong action?**

---

## Week 8 — Bureaucracy: Designing the Impossible

### Concept
**Processes as interfaces**

### Lecture
Students move beyond individual interfaces and consider entire processes as designed experiences.

Examples include:
- University administration
- Government forms
- Insurance
- Refund systems
- Applications
- Customer support

Topics include:
- Repeated information
- Conflicting instructions
- Unclear responsibility
- Excessive steps
- Waiting
- Dependencies

### Tutorial — The Maze
Students attempt to complete an intentionally terrible administrative process.

The process contains unnecessary forms, unclear terminology, conflicting instructions, and repeated information.

After completing it, students create a process map showing where friction and failure occur.

### Key Question
> **What if the interface is the entire system?**

---

## Week 9 — Does It Actually Work?

### Concept
**User testing and evidence**

### Lecture
Students learn that claiming an interface is confusing is different from demonstrating that it is confusing.

Topics include:
- User observation
- Task completion
- Completion time
- Error rates
- Abandonment
- User feedback
- Predicted vs actual behaviour

### Tutorial — Human Guinea Pigs
Students test their deliberately bad interface with classmates.

They observe how participants behave without explaining what is supposed to be frustrating.

Students compare their predictions against what actually happened.

### Assessment
**Hostile Design Experiment — 20%**

Students report the results of testing their hostile interface and evaluate whether their design produced the intended behaviour.

---

# Week 10 — When Bad Design Is Good Design

### Concept
**Necessary friction and design trade-offs**

### Lecture
The course challenges one of its own assumptions:

> **Is frustrating design automatically bad design?**

Students examine examples such as:
- Two-factor authentication
- Confirmation dialogs
- Medical warnings
- Airport security
- Safety interlocks
- Speed bumps

These systems intentionally create friction because removing it may create larger problems.

### Tutorial — Defend the Indefensible
Students are assigned a design from the Hate Wall that people generally dislike.

Their task is to defend it.

They must identify legitimate reasons why the design might exist and what could happen if its frustrating elements were removed.

### Key Question
> **Can something be annoying and still be well designed?**

---

## Week 11 — Fix It Without Breaking It

### Concept
**Redesign and trade-offs**

### Lecture
Students learn how to improve a frustrating system without ignoring the reasons it exists.

Topics include:
- User needs
- Business requirements
- Safety
- Technical constraints
- Institutional constraints
- Trade-offs
- Unintended consequences

The goal is not simply to make something easier or prettier.

### Tutorial — Redemption Studio
Students bring the system selected for their final project.

They present:

**Original → Problem → Cause → Constraints → Proposed Redesign**

Other students challenge the redesign by identifying stakeholders or requirements that may have been ignored.

### Key Question
> **Can you make it better without removing what the system actually needs?**

---

## Week 12 — The Worst Design Awards

### Concept
**Synthesis and reflection**

### Lecture
The final lecture returns to the question from Week 1:

> **What actually makes a design bad?**

Students reconsider their original examples using the concepts developed throughout the semester.

The lecture connects:
- Frustration
- Incentives
- Behaviour
- Manipulation
- Friction
- Systems
- Evidence
- Trade-offs

### Tutorial — The Worst Design Awards
Students present their final projects in an informal awards format.

Possible categories include:
- Most Confusing
- Most Manipulative
- Most Unnecessary
- Most Effective
- Most Defensible
- Worst Physical Design
- Worst Process
- Best Redemption

### Assessment
**The Redemption Project — 40%**

Students analyse, defend, test, and redesign a real frustrating system.

---

# Recurring Course Activity — The Hate Wall

The **Hate Wall** runs throughout the semester.

Students continuously add frustrating designs they encounter. Earlier examples are revisited as new concepts are introduced.

An example might develop like this:

```text id="4q6rlb"
Week 1
"This checkout is annoying."

Week 2
"The company benefits from making the alternative harder to choose."

Week 3
"The interface directs attention toward the preferred option."

Week 4
"This could be classified as visual interference."

Week 6
"The alternative contains unnecessary interaction friction."

Week 9
"Testing shows users actually overlook the alternative."

Week 10
"Some of the friction may have a legitimate purpose."

Week 11
"Here is how we could redesign it while preserving that purpose."
```

The Hate Wall gives the course continuity and shows how students' understanding develops over the semester.

# Assessment Progression

The assessments are placed at the end of each three-week stage.

```text id="6fbx76"
WEEKS 1–3
Observe + Analyse
        ↓
HATE CATALOGUE — 15%

WEEKS 4–6
Manipulate + Create
        ↓
THE WORST INTERFACE — 25%

WEEKS 7–9
Expand + Test
        ↓
HOSTILE DESIGN EXPERIMENT — 20%

WEEKS 10–12
Question + Redesign
        ↓
THE REDEMPTION PROJECT — 40%
```

Each assessment requires students to demonstrate the skills developed in the previous weeks rather than introducing an unrelated task.

---

# Overall Course Progression

The semester follows one continuous sequence:

```text id="57lczg"
NOTICE
"This is annoying."
       ↓
ANALYSE
"Why is it annoying?"
       ↓
QUESTION
"Who benefits from this?"
       ↓
UNDERSTAND
"How does the design influence behaviour?"
       ↓
CREATE
"Can I deliberately create that effect?"
       ↓
EXPAND
"Does this apply beyond interfaces?"
       ↓
TEST
"Does it actually affect users?"
       ↓
CHALLENGE
"Is the frustration necessarily bad?"
       ↓
REDESIGN
"Can I make it better?"
```

The aim is for each week to have a **memorable practical activity** rather than relying entirely on lectures. Students should finish the course having experienced, created, tested, defended, and redesigned bad design themselves.
